## Where SportsNegotiation lands

**Fourth, tied with ArtCommission — and second-to-last on revenue.**

| Idea | Base-case revenue |
|---|---|
| 07 DietGrocery | **$30.7M** |
| SportsLayer | **$21.5M** |
| 08 StartupValuation | $1.77M |
| 10 ArtCommission | $1.05M |
| **12 SportsNegotiation** | **$663k** |
| 09 Voting | ~$650k/yr |

Its own model says why: even capturing **4% of every deep-sim PC owner in America plus 12% of every deal-focused sport management student** reaches only **$1.73M**. **The constraint is that the countable population is small** — roughly 305,000 enthusiasts and 20,400 students, against 12.8M for DietGrocery.

### Three things it does better than anything else in the set

1. **The best evidence base of any project except 07.** Steam's API gave **1,921 negative reviews with verified purchase, real dates, playtime and upvote counts** — the only dossier where a complaint comes with "this person has played 1,898 hours." Every other project relied on App Store or Trustpilot reviews with none of that context.
2. **A finite, named institutional buyer list.** 441 sport management programs is more tractable than 87,100 interior designers, 34,553 Form D filers or 114M sports streamers. **It is the only idea where the entire buyer universe could be called in a month.**
3. **The clearest unoccupied mechanic.** Front Office Football Nine **won't let a user construct a trade**; Football Manager negotiates **by text message**. No competitor does propose → counter → revise. Compare ArtCommission, where the "photograph your room" feature already ships at scale.

### Three things that hold it back

1. **Nobody is paid to practice, and nobody pays to be judged.** The research found no employer that recognises a simulator credential and no rubric for scoring a deal. Section 4 therefore models **zero revenue from the credential** — which is the product's whole premise.
2. **Its claim test failed the same way the VotingApp's did.** **CICERO did this in November 2022** — negotiating in natural language by inferring other players' beliefs and intentions, top 10% of an online Diplomacy league, on a 2.7B model. Voting's capability was a **free Google API from 2012**. Both ideas rest on a "newly possible" that wasn't.
3. **The measured failure mode is the product's core verb.** The claim is that AI can *hold firm*. **SYCON-Bench exists because models don't** — the best model tested is sycophantic 29% of the time, and OpenAI shipped and rolled back a sycophantic GPT-4o in four days having run **no evaluation for it**.

## Is the comparison fair?

Four places it isn't:

| | |
|---|---|
| **Evidence channels differ in quality** | SportsNegotiation's Steam data (verified purchase, hours, upvotes) is **stronger than any other dossier's**. 09_Voting had **32 reviews**, padded with 119 from a news app; 10_Art used interior-design apps as proxies. Scoring "complaint volume" across these is not like-for-like. |
| **Price points were chosen, not derived** | $19.99/mo, $1,000/yr, $4.99/mo, 15% of GMV, free-with-referrals, and $100/yr. SportsNegotiation's $100 sits **above every competing product's one-time price** ($29.99–$69.99) and far below professional training — it is the most out-of-band price in the set. |
| **Revenue units differ** | Four are subscriptions, ArtCommission is a take rate on GMV, SportsLayer is third-party referral fees. SportsNegotiation is the only one that **splits its own model across three separate populations** (students, enthusiasts, institutions), which makes its total look more engineered than the others. |
| **Softest number varies in kind** | SportsNegotiation's is a **×30 Steam-reviews-to-owners heuristic** — a rule of thumb, not a measurement. 08's was an unverified ×3 attempt multiplier; 10's an invented ×40%. All three are load-bearing and none is checkable without primary research. |

## What I would do with this

**It doesn't change the decision.** 07_DietGroceryApp still leads on every criterion that has evidence behind it, and by an order of magnitude on revenue.

**But SportsNegotiation is the cheapest of the six to kill or confirm**, because both of its decisive questions can be answered in a week without code:

1. **The hold-firm test** — 20 scripted negotiations where refusing is correct; push hard; count how often the AI caves. Above ~10% and the product teaches the wrong lesson.
2. **The 441-program call list** — ask ten of them whether they would grade with it. That single answer decides whether the institutional path (the only one that scales here) exists at all.

If both come back positive, the ranking is worth revisiting. If either fails, it can be closed out permanently for about a week of work — which is more than can be said for the other five.
