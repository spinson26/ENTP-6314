# Testing the Claim: "AI-Generated Fine-Art Briefs Make This Newly Possible"

**Prepared:** September 22, 2026
**Window searched:** March 2025 – September 2026, plus the historical record needed to test counterargument 1.

## The claim, stated precisely

> An art commission app is **newly possible** because AI can generate a **fine-art brief, in text**, describing what a customer may want, which the artist can then change in the final artwork.

**Verdict up front: a brief is words, and words have never been the constraint.** Commission briefs have been collected by forms for fifteen years and compiled by paid professionals for decades. What *is* new is narrower — and, importantly, a January 2025 legal ruling makes your specific configuration the clean one. Stated at the end.

---

## Part 1 — Evidence FOR

### 1a. The visual half got cheap and consistent — August 2025

Google released **Gemini 2.5 Flash Image ("nano banana") on August 26, 2025**: it holds a subject's appearance across multiple edits, performs targeted local edits by natural-language instruction, and **fuses multiple input images** — at roughly **$0.039 per image**. ([Google Developers Blog](https://developers.googleblog.com/en/introducing-gemini-2-5-flash-image/))

Fusing a room photo with three inspiration uploads into a coherent visual reference was unreliable before mid-2025; edit-to-edit consistency was the specific failure mode. **That is a real, dated capability change** — though note it improves *images*, and your claim is about *text*.

### 1b. The legal line drawn in January 2025 favors your exact design

On **January 29, 2025**, the US Copyright Office concluded that **prompting alone does not create authorship**, but works where AI is an **assistive tool** and a human supplies the expression **are** protectable. ([Copyright Office](https://copyright.gov/ai/); [Jones Day](https://www.jonesday.com/en/insights/2025/02/copyrightability-of-ai-outputs-us-copyright-office-analyzes-human-authorship-requirement))

**"AI writes the brief, the human paints the painting" lands squarely on the protectable side.** This is the strongest single argument in the claim's favor, and it is dated, official and verifiable.

### 1c. Your buyer-side channel already works this way

- **83% of creative professionals** use generative AI at work; **72% of designers** use it day to day; **40% use it daily**; **33% generate design assets** with it. ([Figma design statistics](https://www.figma.com/resource-library/design-statistics/); [Envato State of AI in Creative Work 2026](https://elements.envato.com/learn/ai-trend-report))
- Section 3 identified **interior designers (87,100)** as the most concentrated buyer. **They are already AI-native.** An AI-assisted brief is not a culture shock to them.

### 1d. Buyer appetite for AI-adjacent art exists

**28% of emerging buyers have already purchased AI-generated art and 52% expect to.** ([Hiscox Art and AI Report 2024](https://www.hiscoxgroup.com/news/press-releases/2024/19-09-24))

## Part 2 — Evidence AGAINST

### 2a. The platforms where commission artists actually live ban AI involvement

| Platform | Policy |
|---|---|
| **VGen** (commission marketplace, tagline "For the love of human creativity") | **"Until generative AI is made with Consent, Credit, and Compensation, it is not welcome."** Accounts using AI artwork, heavy tracing or stolen assets **may be suspended** ([VGen AI Policy](https://help.vgen.co/hc/en-us/articles/12882944163991-AI-Policy)) |
| **Artfinder** | **AI-generated artwork explicitly prohibited** ([artfinder.com/sell](https://www.artfinder.com/sell/)) |
| **Cara** | Built on AI refusal; grew **40,000 → 650,000+ users in one week** (June 2024) after Meta's training announcement ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2024/06/06/a-social-app-for-creatives-cara-grew-from-40k-to-650k-users-in-a-week-because-artists-are-fed-up-with-metas-ai-policies/)) |
| **Artfol** | Brands itself "AI-Free Art Community" |
| **Etsy** | Mandatory AI disclosure wherever AI touched the process |

**Your supply side is 347,000 people with a median art income of $20,000–$30,000 (Section 1), organised on platforms that suspend accounts for AI involvement.** Capability is not what stands between you and this market.

### 2b. Buyers want AI kept visible and priced lower

**82% of collectors want a clear distinction between AI-made and human-made art, and only 16% of seasoned collectors believe AI art will reach the value of human work.** ([Hiscox](https://www.hiscoxgroup.com/news/press-releases/2024/19-09-24)) Your premise includes buyers who want **art that appreciates** — the same survey says those buyers discount anything AI-touched.

### 2c. The tools that do this today are reviewed badly at exactly your feature

- **Artist.ai, 1★, Sept 2 2026** — bad at generating from **reference images**, which is your inspiration-upload feature.
- **Renovate AI, 1★, Jul 30 2026** — the image **degrades with every edit**.
- **Home AI, 2★, Sept 18 2026** — a free general assistant (Gemini) does the job better than the paid vertical app.

### 2d. Nobody has ever measured whether an AI brief produces better art

I searched for benchmarks on brief quality, commission satisfaction or artist-output ratings from AI-assisted versus human-written briefs. **None exists.** Every AI benchmark in this space measures image generation, not briefing. **The central premise of your claim is unmeasured — not disproven, unmeasured.** `[UNVERIFIED — absence of evidence.]`

### 2e. The legal overhang has not cleared

**Andersen v. Stability AI** — the visual artists' class action — had class-certification briefing completed in **April 2026** and remains active ([BakerHostetler](https://www.bakerlaw.com/andersen-v-stability-ai/)). If any AI-generated *imagery* reaches the artist and is copied into the work, the Copyright Office framework leaves the protectability of that portion doubtful.

## Part 3 — Counterargument 1, argued at full strength

### **"This was already possible five years ago."**

**A brief is a piece of writing. Buyers have been writing them since patrons wrote to painters.**

**The form existed:** Fiverr launched in 2010 with brief-driven ordering, revisions and escrow; Etsy custom orders have collected buyer specifications for years; DeviantArt-era commission sheets and VGen's structured commission forms do the same. **Size, palette, subject, deadline, reference images — these are form fields, and they have been form fields for fifteen years.**

**The inspiration upload existed:** Pinterest launched in 2010. **The mood board is sixteen years old** and remains the default way buyers communicate taste.

**The professional version existed and was priced:** art advisors and consultants have compiled briefs from a client's space, taste and budget for decades, at **5–20% of the artwork** (Section 2). Art buyers and art producers do it on salary at **$74,000–$81,000/yr**. **This is not an unsolved problem; it is a solved problem with a price.**

**And the visualization existed:** wall-preview apps — Smartist, Artrooms, ArtStage, iArtView — have let buyers see art at size on a photographed wall since roughly 2020, with thousands of ratings each.

**The strong form:** every component you describe shipped before 2021. What you are adding is that an LLM drafts the paragraph instead of the buyer typing it or the consultant writing it. **That is a convenience improvement on a form field — not an enabling condition.** And the market's failure was never that briefs were hard to write; it was that commissioning is high-friction, high-trust and hard to complete (Section 2).

**Where this argument is weakest:** pre-LLM briefs required the buyer to already possess the vocabulary. Most people cannot say "a muted ochre palette with impasto texture at 48×36 to balance a north-facing wall" — they can only say "something warm, I guess." **Turning a room photograph and three saved images into artist-usable specification language is genuinely new**, and it is the one part of your claim that survives this counterargument.

## Part 4 — Counterargument 2, argued at full strength

### **"This still is not possible."**

**1. The supply side has pre-emptively banned you.** VGen suspends accounts for AI involvement. Artfinder prohibits AI art. Cara and Artfol exist as refusals. You are proposing an AI-mediated product to the single most AI-hostile professional community in the economy, and your model requires them to accept briefs from it.

**2. The buyer premise contradicts itself.** Your premise wants art that **appreciates in value**. 82% of collectors want AI involvement disclosed and only 16% of seasoned collectors think AI-touched work will hold value. **Disclosing AI involvement in the brief plausibly reduces the appreciation the buyer came for.** `[Inference — no study isolates the effect of an AI-written *brief*, as opposed to AI-generated art, on resale value. Nobody has measured it.]`

**3. The premise misdiagnoses the artist's problem.** "Artist's block" is offered as a driver. Section 1 found artists' measured constraints are **income ($20–30k median), instability (80%+ report unstable earnings), and reach** — not a shortage of ideas. **You are solving the problem the artists don't have and not solving the one they do.**

**4. The real failure mode is completion, not briefing.** Section 2: 14% of complaints in this category are trust and scams; a Fiverr buyer lost $600 to a vetted, highly-rated seller. Section 4: at a 15% take on completed transactions you'd absorb ~1,200 failed commissions a year. **A better brief does not make a stranger finish a painting.**

**5. Nothing has been measured.** No benchmark, study or product exists demonstrating that AI-assisted briefs produce better commissioned art, higher completion rates or happier buyers.

**Where this argument is weakest:** it attacks *AI-generated imagery* and *AI involvement in the artwork*. **It does not defeat a text-only brief generator that never produces an image.** VGen's objection is to AI *art*; Artfinder prohibits AI *artwork*. A tool that writes a specification in words — and can prove no generated image touched the piece — is arguably outside every one of those policies. `[UNVERIFIED — none of the policies I read explicitly addresses AI-written briefs. That ambiguity is a risk, not a permission.]`

## Part 5 — What survives

The claim overstates novelty and understates the obstacle. Here is the version that survives:

> **Not newly possible — newly legible, and newly legally clean.** Briefs are as old as patronage and have been form fields since 2010. Two things changed. **(1) Legibility:** since mid-2025, a room photograph plus a few inspiration images can be turned into structured specification language — palette, scale, texture, theme — at about four cents, which non-experts could never write themselves. **(2) Legal position:** the Copyright Office's January 29, 2025 framework makes "AI assists, human authors" the protectable configuration, so a text brief followed by a human-painted work is the clean way to build this. **What has not changed is that the artists you need have banned AI involvement, that collectors discount AI-touched work, and that commissions fail on trust and completion rather than on briefing.** The product that survives generates **text only, never imagery**, says so publicly, and sells to interior designers — who already use AI daily — rather than to artists, who don't.

### Four tests that would falsify this — cheapest first

1. **The VGen test (free, one week).** Ask 20 working commission artists: *does an AI-written brief disqualify you or your work from platforms you use?* If a meaningful share say yes, **your supply side is gated before you build anything.**
2. **The blind brief test.** Give 10 artists AI-written briefs and 10 human-written briefs, blinded, and have buyers rate the resulting work. **Nobody has run this. It is the central premise of your claim and it is unmeasured.**
3. **The no-image test.** Build it text-only. If the value proposition collapses without generated imagery, you are an AI art product wearing a commission app's coat — and every policy in Part 2 applies to you.
4. **The designer test.** Ask 20 interior designers what they actually want: a brief generator, or a vetted artist roster with a delivery guarantee? Sections 2 and 4 both say their pain is **completion**, not briefing.
