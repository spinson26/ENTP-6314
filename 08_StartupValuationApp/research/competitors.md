## Per-platform notes

### Carta — the default, and the most expensive to leave
Carta is an equity *administration* system that sells valuations as an attached service, not a valuation product. Its free Launch tier (≤25 stakeholders, ≤$1M raised) is a deliberate funnel: the moment you raise a real round, you're into contact-sales pricing that reviewers describe in the thousands. It is also the only platform here that is an SEC-registered transfer agent — a genuine moat that has nothing to do with valuation quality.

### Equidam — the closest thing to this project that already exists
Five-method valuation, founder-facing, published prices, free tier, benchmark data, and a $1,990 409A option. **If you build a founder-facing valuation app, this is your direct competitor** — and its review base (15 lifetime, 2 in the past year) says almost nobody is using it. That is either the opening or the warning, and the same ambiguity flagged in Section 2. `[UNVERIFIED: no usage data beyond their marketing claim of 140,000+ founders served]`

### Valutico — proof the professional tier is a different market
28 techniques, a serious comps database, quote-only pricing, and a reviewer base of bankers and valuation analysts. A 5★ reviewer states plainly it suits larger enterprises rather than small business. **Not a competitor for founders — a reminder that the good comps data lives where founders can't afford it.**

### Eqvista — the price floor for compliance
$990/yr for a pre-revenue 409A *with unlimited updates and a cap table* is the number that any valuation-adjacent product has to beat or route around. It also shows where the category is heading: valuation as a subscription commodity, not a per-report engagement.

### Crunchbase — has already shipped the investor-matching half
Ranked investor matches off predictive funding signals, $588/yr, free tier for discovery. The product risk for this project is not that matching is hard — it's that a $588/yr incumbent with 4M company profiles already does it. The opening is their reputation: **1.4★, with billing and data-accuracy complaints dominating.**

### PitchBook — the data everyone wants, at a price founders won't pay
$15k–$20k for a seat, owned by a public company, no published price, 1.7★ public reputation. Founders are not customers and will never be. **Any product that gives founders defensible comps at a founder price is competing with PitchBook's data, not its business model.**

### Foundersuite — the one built for the founder, and the one with real reviews
$745–$1,609/yr, 227,000 investors, free tier, 110 Capterra reviews at 4.7★. Its gaps are precise and documented: **incomplete investor data, no cap table, no valuation.**

## What does nobody serve well?

**1. The negotiation number.** Every platform here produces either a *compliance* number (409A — Carta, Eqvista, Equidam's $1,990 tier) or a *professional* number (Valutico, for advisors valuing someone else's company). **Nobody produces the number a founder argues for in a room** — the pre-money they should ask for, with comparables attached, at their stage, sector and geography. The 409A is deliberately lower than the preferred-share price; handing a founder their 409A as "your valuation" is actively misleading.

**2. Valuation joined to investor targeting.** Carta and Eqvista know your equity and nothing about investors. Crunchbase and PitchBook know investors and nothing about you. Foundersuite has the investors but **no cap table and no valuation** — its own reviewers say so. **No product in this set answers "here's what you're worth, here's who funds companies worth that, at your stage, in your sector."** That specific join is empty.

**3. Continuous valuation.** Everything here is point-in-time: a 409A is annual, Equidam runs a 12-month valuation cycle, Valutico is engagement-based. **Nobody answers "what am I worth today?" continuously as revenue, headcount and the market move** — which is precisely what the project proposes.

**4. The pre-institutional founder.** Carta's free tier caps at $1M raised. PitchBook starts at $15,000. Valutico is built for enterprises. Crunchbase is sold to salespeople. **The 40,000 annual Shark Tank applicants, the 1.7M high-propensity new businesses, and the founders raising their first $250k have no product in this comparison built for them** — they have spreadsheets.

**5. Published outcomes.** Not one of these platforms publishes how many of its users actually raised money. Section 2 showed that's exactly the grievance that turns paying users hostile ("I applied to 60 programs and got nothing").

**6. Honest pricing.** **Three of seven don't publish a price at all**, and the two that dominate the category are rated 1.4★ and 1.7★ largely over billing conduct. In a market this opaque, a published price is a product feature.

**7. Non-equity capital paths.** All seven assume a priced equity round. None routes a founder toward revenue-based financing, grants, SBA lending or crowdfunding when that's the better fit — even though most of the 5.67M annual business applicants will never be VC-fundable.

### The sharpest wedge

**Continuous, defensible, founder-facing valuation joined to ranked investor targeting, at a published price under $1,000/year, for companies below the institutional threshold.** Every element of that sentence is empty in the table above. The two hard parts are comparables data (PitchBook's moat) and proving outcomes (nobody's doing it, and it's what users punish you for).
